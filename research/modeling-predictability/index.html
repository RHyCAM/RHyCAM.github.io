Research / Theme

Modeling & Predictability

Hydroclimate modeling, downscaling, and Earth system predictability

Illustration

Overview

Understanding and predicting hydroclimate extremes depends on tools that represent atmospheric and hydrologic processes in enough detail to capture individual events. We build and evaluate such tools, from kilometer-scale atmospheric simulations to machine-learning frameworks, and use them to push the limits of what can be predicted.

Research directions

  • Convection-permitting and Earth system modeling using WRF, MPAS-A, and E3SM.
  • Evaluate dynamically and statistically downscaled climate products for hydrologic applications.
  • Develop and apply AI/ML methods for atmospheric and hydrologic modeling and prediction.
  • Assess model sensitivity and uncertainty, and investigate hydroclimate predictability across scales.

J. Geophys. Res.: Atmos., 128 (2023)

Wildfire Typing Improves Wildfire Predictions in the Western United States

Machine-learning clustering identifies distinct weather and moisture conditions associated with wildfires in the western U.S., improving seasonal predictions by 10%. Historically, some wildfire types have grown more common while those associated with wetter soils have declined, revealing changing patterns of wildfire risk.

J. Hydrometeor., 22 (2021)

Contrasting Responses of Mountain Snowpack to Warmer Sea Surfaces

Warmer coastal ocean temperatures bring wetter, warmer winters to the U.S. West Coast, reducing snowpack in the northern Cascades while increasing it in the southern Sierra Nevada. Changes in atmospheric rivers and precipitation interact with elevation to produce these contrasting responses.

Warmer Sea Surfaces Intensify Landfalling Atmospheric Rivers

Warmer coastal ocean temperatures strengthen landfalling atmospheric rivers along the U.S. West Coast, increasing their frequency, intensity, and spatial extent. Geophys. Res. Lett., 45 (2018)

Bracing for an Atmospheric River — and a Sea of Rain

Atmospheric river occurrence, intensity, and duration help predict extreme precipitation in western U.S. watersheds at daily and monthly timescales. Machine-learning analysis highlights the importance of intense, long-lasting events, with stronger atmospheric rivers generally associated with heavier precipitation.
