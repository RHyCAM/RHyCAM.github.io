Research / Theme

Hydroclimate Extremes

Atmospheric processes, hydroclimate extremes, and compound hazards

Illustration

Overview

Storms, floods, heatwaves, and wildfires pose major risks to communities and infrastructure. We study how these events form, how they have changed over the past century, and how they may evolve in a warmer world — knowledge needed to plan for and adapt to them.

Research directions

  • Historical trends and variability in storms, floods, heatwaves, and wildfires using observations, reanalysis, and model simulations.
  • Projected changes in individual and compound extremes under future climate scenarios and their implications for risk.
  • Event-scale structure of hydroclimate extremes and its response to changing environmental conditions.

What we have learned

Nature Climate Change, 13 (2023)

Wettest Winter Storms in the Western U.S. Growing Wetter

The strongest winter storms in the western U.S. could bring up to 40% more precipitation at storm centers by mid-century. Understanding these changes is important for assessing future storm risks and improving infrastructure planning.

See AP news, SF Chronicle, PNNL news release

Read the story · Paper

J. Geophys. Res.: Atmos., 128 (2023)

Wildfire Typing Improves Wildfire Predictions in the Western United States

Machine-learning clustering identifies distinct weather and moisture conditions associated with wildfires in the western U.S., improving seasonal predictions by 10%. Historically, some wildfire types have grown more common while those associated with wetter soils have declined, revealing changing patterns of wildfire risk.

See DOE highlight, PNNL highlight

Geophys. Res. Lett., 50 (2023)

Footprint of Large-Scale Climate Variabilities on Regional Weather Systems

WAC-hydro classifies daily weather into 12 types based on precipitation and temperature, connecting large-scale climate variability to snowpack, runoff, and floods in the Pacific Northwest. The framework identifies weather patterns that elevate flood risk through heavy rainfall, snowmelt, or both.
