The wettest winter storms in the western U.S. are getting sharper

By mid-century, the strongest cold-season storms could bring up to 40% more precipitation, concentrated toward their centers — with direct consequences for how infrastructure is designed

The question

Winter storms in the western U.S. cause billions of dollars in damages. How will the precipitation they deliver — and how it is distributed within each storm — change as the climate warms?

Key findings

  1. By mid-century, the strongest winter storms could bring up to 40% more precipitation.
  2. The increase is not uniform: more precipitation falls near the storm centers, so storms become spatially “sharper”.
  3. If these structural changes are not accounted for, infrastructure planning could misjudge future storm risks.

Engineers size dams, spillways, and drainage systems using the precipitation a storm deposits over a watershed. That depends not only on how much falls in total, but also on how it is distributed in space and time. This study looked at both, treating each cold-season storm as an object with its own footprint and internal structure.
